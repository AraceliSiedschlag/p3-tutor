Warm-up 1 min

Recall

With cats, you learned to read signals the cat was already giving you, like a slow blink or flattened ears. Here's the flip side: how do you think you'd get a dog to do something it has never done before, like spin in a circle?

Check your answer

You can't just wait for a spin to happen on its own and hope. Trainers reward tiny pieces of the behavior, one step closer each time, until the full trick shows up. That's the whole lesson in one sentence, read on for how it actually works.

The mechanism: reward what you want, right away 4 min

Dogs don't learn by being told what a word means. They learn by noticing what happens right after they do something. This is operant conditioning: a behavior followed by a good consequence happens more often, and a behavior followed by nothing or something bad happens less (Companion Animal Psychology, summarizing AVSAB's 2021 position statement). You've basically got this already, since it's the same cause-and-effect logic you used to read a cat's slow blink, just running the other direction.

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior reviewed the research and came down firmly on one side. Reward-based training gives "the most advantages and least harm to the learner's welfare," with no exception even for difficult behavior, and no real role left for correction-based methods, such as leash jerks, prong collars, or shock collars (Companion Animal Psychology, summarizing AVSAB's 2021 position statement).

Check yourself

A friend says their trainer uses a firm leash correction whenever the dog pulls, and swears it's the only way to get a reliable result. Based on what AVSAB's review found, what's the best response?

Show the answer

Reward-based training comes out ahead on both welfare and effectiveness, with no exception made for a hard-to-fix behavior like pulling. That's the finding, not just one trainer's opinion.

Once you're rewarding, timing is everything. A reward tells the dog exactly which action just worked, but only if it lands fast: within roughly a second or two of the action. Wait three seconds to hand over the treat and the dog has already moved on to sniffing the floor, so now you've rewarded floor-sniffing, not the thing you meant to (Furever K9, Timing in Dog Training).

That's why trainers use a marker, a clicker or a sharp verbal word like "yes," which they can fire the instant the right thing happens, with the treat following a beat later. The marker is the precise timestamp; the treat is just the payment that comes after (AKC, Clicker Training Dogs).

Before the marker means anything, you have to "charge" it: click, then treat, about 10 to 20 times with nothing else going on, so the dog learns click equals treat is coming. Skip this and the click is just noise (AKC, Clicker Training Dogs).
A common mix-up

It's tempting to think a dog needs to "understand" the command word first, the way you'd explain a rule to a person. It's backwards: the word is the last thing added, once the behavior already happens reliably. Trainers attach the cue after the behavior exists, not before, the same way you'd name a keyboard shortcut after you've already built the feature it triggers, not before.

A common mix-up

It's also tempting to think treats are "bribery," that a dog trained this way only obeys when food is visible. That's a fading problem, not a reinforcement problem: fading is gradually thinning out how often treats show up, swapping in praise, play, or life rewards, until only occasional food is needed once a behavior is solid. The method isn't bribery, it's just under-faded if the dog won't work without a treat in sight.

Check yourself

Your dog sits. You say "good dog" and reach into your pocket, fish out a treat, and hand it over four seconds later. What most likely got reinforced?

Show the answer

Whatever the dog was doing four seconds after it sat is the behavior that got reinforced, since that's what was happening the instant the reward landed. A gap of a second or two is the target, not four.

Shaping: how to build a trick nobody taught the dog yet 3 min

Shaping is how you get a behavior that has never happened before: break the final trick into small steps, and reward only the step the dog is currently on, raising the bar once that step is solid. The American Kennel Club lays out the rule plainly: once a step is reliable, "stop rewarding the step one behavior and only reinforce step two," watching for small variations that move toward the goal and marking those (AKC, What Is Shaping?).

If you've ever tested a recipe by tweaking one variable at a time, salt, then heat, then time, until it's right, shaping is the same idea applied to a living, moving animal: small adjustment, check the result, adjust again.

Check yourself

You've been shaping "spin" for a week. The dog now reliably turns a full circle. You're still marking and treating every quarter-turn, the step you started with. What should you do?

Show the answer

Stop rewarding quarter-turns and only reward bigger turns now. Raising the criterion once a step is solid is what moves the dog toward the full behavior instead of parking it on the easy version.

Worked example and practice 2 min

Worked example: shaping "touch" (nose to your hand)

Goal: the dog touches its nose to your open palm on cue.

  1. Hold your palm a few inches from the dog's nose. The moment it even glances toward your hand, mark and treat, because any interest at all is the first approximation.
  2. Once glancing is reliable, raise the bar: only mark a lean toward the hand, not just a glance, so the dog has to try harder for the same reward.
  3. Once leaning is reliable, only mark actual nose contact with the palm, moving the target a little farther each time it succeeds, until contact at a distance is solid.
  4. Only now add the word: say "touch" right before the dog is about to make contact, so the word and the action line up, then start saying it before you present your hand at all.
Your turn

Shape "spin" (a full turn in place). First step given: mark and treat any turn of the head toward one side, even a small one. Work out steps two through four yourself before you look.

Show a solution

Step 2: mark only when the head turn pulls the shoulders and front feet around with it. Step 3: mark only a half-turn of the whole body. Step 4: mark only a full 360-degree turn, then add the word "spin" right before the dog turns, once the full turn is reliable without the word.

Summary 1 min

  • Dogs learn from what happens right after an action, within about a second, not from being told a rule in words.
  • A marker (clicker or word) timestamps the exact right moment; the treat is just the payment that follows.
  • Shaping builds a brand-new behavior by rewarding small steps toward it, and dropping the reward for a step once the next one shows up.
  • The cue word is added last, once the behavior is already reliable, not first.

Say it in your own words: without looking back, explain to yourself why rewarding the same easy step forever would stall a dog's progress on a trick.
