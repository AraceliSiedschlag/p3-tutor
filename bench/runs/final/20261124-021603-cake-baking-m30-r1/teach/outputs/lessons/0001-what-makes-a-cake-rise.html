What Makes a Cake Rise

By the end of this lesson, you can explain what actually makes a cake rise, tell baking soda apart from baking powder, and work out the most likely reason a cake sank or came out flat.

Why this matters for you

A cake that sinks in the middle is one of the two outcomes you most want to avoid, right alongside a dry one. Today you get the chemistry behind that collapse, so you can see it coming and fix it before it ruins a cake you were proud of.

Warm-up 2 min

Recall

Last session, you learned to weigh your flour, or spoon it loosely into the cup and level it off, instead of scooping straight from the bag. Why does that matter?

Check your answer

Scooping packs extra flour into the cup, and too much flour is one of the main reasons a cake comes out dry. Hold onto that idea: today you'll see the same pattern — too much of an ingredient wrecking the result — show up again with leavening, except it causes sinking rather than dryness.

Three things make a cake rise 5 min

A box mix hides a lot from you. Flour, leaveners, even stabilizers all come pre-measured in that pouch, so the rise just happens and you never had to think about why. Scratch baking hands all three of those jobs back to you, starting with the main one: what actually lifts a cake as it bakes.

The general word for anything that makes a batter rise is leavening: anything that creates or expands gas bubbles inside the batter so it puffs up in the oven. A cake rises from three leavening sources working together, not from one single ingredient doing it all.

The first source is chemical gas. Baking soda and baking powder are chemical leaveners: once mixed into wet batter, they trigger an acid-base reaction that releases carbon dioxide (CO2) gas, and those bubbles act like thousands of tiny balloons scattered through the batter (NC State University News; Scientific Baking). In plain terms: an acid is a sour, reactive compound (think vinegar or lemon juice) and a base is its chemical opposite; mix the two and they react, and in this case that reaction throws off CO2 as a byproduct.

The second source is steam and beaten-in air. Water in the batter turns to steam as the oven heats it, and steam takes up far more room than liquid water, so it expands every bubble already sitting in the batter. Air you beat into eggs, or cream into butter and sugar, adds even more of those starter bubbles for the steam and CO2 to inflate (Fat Daddio's).

The third source is structure. As the cake heats through, the proteins in the eggs and in the flour's gluten — the stretchy protein network that forms once flour gets wet and mixed — firm up permanently — a process called coagulation — and that locks the risen shape in place. Without it, every bubble you inflated would simply collapse the moment the cake left the oven (Fat Daddio's).

This is why a box mix feels foolproof: it's pre-balanced so the chemical gas, the moisture for steam, and the protein for structure already sit in the right ratios. In your own batter, you're the one keeping that balance.
A common mix-up

It's tempting to think baking soda or baking powder does all the work, since it's the ingredient recipes call "the leavener." But a perfectly leavened batter with no egg or gluten structure to hold it would still collapse, and a batter with great structure but no gas would never rise in the first place. All three pieces have to show up.

Baking soda needs an acid 4 min

Baking soda is one specific chemical: sodium bicarbonate, a base. A base cannot react on its own — it needs an acid to react with before it releases any gas. Dry baking soda sitting in a bowl, with no acid nearby, does nothing (NC State University News).

That acid has to come from somewhere else in the recipe. Buttermilk, yogurt, sour cream, vinegar, citrus juice, molasses, brown sugar, and cocoa powder are all acidic enough to react with baking soda and set the CO2-releasing reaction going (NC State University News; Scientific Baking).

If a recipe calls for baking soda but doesn't carry enough acid to match it, the soda just sits there unreacted. The cake rises less than it should, and any soda that never reacted can leave a soapy, bitter, or "chemical" aftertaste (Scientific Baking).

This is why a vanilla cake recipe calling for baking soda almost always calls for buttermilk too, not plain milk. The buttermilk isn't there for tang alone: its acidity is what wakes the soda up and lets it do its job.

Check yourself

A vanilla cake recipe lists: flour, 1 cup buttermilk, 1 tsp baking soda, sugar, eggs. Why does the recipe use buttermilk instead of plain milk?

Show the answer

Buttermilk supplies the acid that reacts with the baking soda — that's the option about the CO2 being released. Without enough acid, the soda can't release its gas, and the cake under-rises and can taste soapy.

Baking powder brings its own acid, in two stages 4 min

Baking powder solves the acid problem by packing its own acid inside the can. It's baking soda, plus a powdered acid, plus a starch filler that keeps the two apart and dry until you add liquid. Because the acid is already there, baking powder reacts with moisture alone — it needs no separate acidic ingredient in the recipe (King Arthur Baking; NC State University News).

Most baking powder sold today is double-acting: it carries two different acids that react at two different times. A fast acid dissolves and reacts the moment it touches liquid, right there in your mixing bowl. A second, slower acid stays inert until the batter actually heats up in the oven, then reacts again (Scientific Baking; NC State University News).

Think of it like two triggers in a scheduling system, since you already live in that world at work. One trigger fires immediately on an event: batter gets wet, first acid reacts right away. The other waits for a condition instead — oven heat — and only reacts once the batter actually gets hot. Your vanilla batter can sit on the dorm counter for a few minutes without going flat, because most of its lift is still waiting on that second, heat-triggered stage.

A common mix-up

Baking soda and baking powder are not interchangeable, cup for cup. Soda is roughly three times stronger than powder, and it has no built-in acid, so swapping one for the other without adjusting both the amount and the recipe's acid will either under-leaven the batter or leave it tasting bitter (King Arthur Baking).

Check yourself

Why can batter made with baking powder sit on the counter a few minutes before baking, while batter made with only baking soda (plus an acid) starts losing lift right away?

Show the answer

Baking powder's fast acid reacts right on mixing, same as soda, but its second, slower acid is still waiting for oven heat. That reserved second stage is why the batter survives sitting out for a bit.

More isn't safer, and sinking isn't always "too little" 4 min

You already nailed the hard part of measuring — weighing or spooning-and-leveling your flour instead of scooping, so you never over-pack a cup (learning record 0001). That instinct, "more of an ingredient is not automatically better," is exactly the instinct leavening needs too.

More leavener is not a safety margin

It's tempting to think a little extra baking soda or powder is a safe way to guarantee a tall cake. It's actually backwards: too much leavener makes gas form and escape faster than the egg and flour structure can trap it (Scientific Baking; NC State University News).

With too much gas, bubbles merge into bigger and bigger pockets instead of staying small and even. Those oversized bubbles burst before the structure has set, so the cake rises fast, then falls — often with a bitter or soapy aftertaste left behind from leavener that never got to react cleanly (Scientific Baking).

Too much flour dries a cake out; too much leavener collapses it. Same rule, different ingredient: an amount added past what the recipe actually needs changes the result for the worse, not the better.

A common mix-up

"A little extra leavening can't hurt, and might even help it rise more" feels logical, since more gas sounds like more lift. In practice, excess gas escapes before the structure can trap it, so the cake balloons up, collapses, and often tastes soapy — the opposite of "safer."

Check yourself: Cake A

This cake domed up fast in the oven, then sank in the middle, and has a faint bitter, soapy aftertaste. What's the most likely cause?

Show the answer

Too much leavener. The fast dome followed by a collapse, plus the bitter aftertaste, both point to excess gas outrunning the structure.

Sinking does not always mean "too little"

When a cake sinks, it's tempting to assume it needed more leavening, since sinking looks like "it didn't rise enough." Often the true cause is the reverse: too much leavener (Scientific Baking) or underbaking (King Arthur Baking) are both more common culprits than too little.

Another common mix-up

"If it sank, it must not have had enough leavening" feels like the obvious read, since sinking looks like a lack of rise. But a sunken cake is just as often the sign of too much leavener, or of a structure that never got the chance to set — not a shortage of gas at all.

Check yourself: Cake B

This cake barely rose at all. It's dense, flat on top, and tastes a little soapy. What's the most likely cause?

Show the answer

Too little acid. Not enough acid to react with the soda means little gas forms, so the cake stays dense, and unreacted soda leaves a soapy edge.

Structure and oven conditions: what actually goes wrong 4 min

Even with exactly the right amount of leavener and the right acid, a cake can still sink if the protein structure never gets the chance to set. Remember, that structure is what locks the risen shape in place once the gas has done its job, and it needs enough time at the right heat to finish (Fat Daddio's).

Underbaking is the most direct version of this: pull the cake out before the structure has fully set, and it can look perfectly fine coming out of the oven, then sink as it cools and the half-set structure gives way (King Arthur Baking).

Oven temperature plays into this too, because ovens are notoriously inaccurate: a dial reading 350°F can really be running quite a bit hotter or cooler inside (King Arthur Baking). If it's actually running cooler than the recipe assumes, the bake effectively takes longer than the recipe's stated time says — so pulling it at the recipe's clock time risks the exact underbaking problem above, just hidden by a crust that looks done. Run hotter, and the edges and top firm up before the middle has had the chance to rise fully (Fat Daddio's).

Opening the oven door mid-bake compounds the same risk: King Arthur Baking notes that opening the door can drop the oven's real temperature by as much as 25°F in an instant (King Arthur Baking) — right when the center may still need that heat to finish setting, so a cake that was otherwise on track can lose its structure's last chance to firm up.

One more cause worth ruling out: old leavener. An America's Test Kitchen test found a 10-month-old can of baking powder raised biscuits to only about half the height of a fresh one, and they "did not rise properly" (America's Test Kitchen). If a box has sat in the back of the cupboard a long time, weak gas production is worth checking before you blame anything else.

So a sunk or flat cake usually traces back to one of these common, fixable causes:

  • Underbaking — pulled before the structure had fully set.
  • An oven running hotter or cooler than its dial claims.
  • Opening the door too early, mid-bake.
  • Too much or too little leavener for the recipe.
  • Leavener that's simply gone stale.

Not some mysterious one-off bad luck.

Your dorm kitchen has one oven and no backup plan, so the temptation to crack the door and peek is real. An inexpensive oven thermometer tells you what the oven is actually doing, since the dial on the front is often off by a surprising amount (King Arthur Baking). Check progress through the window and the oven light instead of opening the door before the last few minutes.

Worked example and practice 4 min

Worked example

A friend's recipe used buttermilk, baking soda, and baking powder together. It domed up fast in the oven, then sank in the middle as it cooled, and tasted faintly bitter. Here's a four-question checklist to find out why.

  1. Is there enough acid for the soda? Yes — the recipe includes buttermilk, which is acidic enough to react with the baking soda. This check is clear, so the cause isn't a shortage of acid.
  2. Was the amount of leavening reasonable — the recipe's own amounts, with no swap and no "extra for safety"? Turns out the friend added a bit more baking powder than the recipe called for, wanting extra insurance against a flat cake. This check is not clear.
  3. Was the oven actually at the temperature the recipe assumed, checked with a thermometer rather than just the dial? Checked and confirmed accurate. This check is clear.
  4. Did the structure get an undisturbed, full bake to finish setting — no underbaking, no door opened early? Yes — baked the full time, door never opened. This check is clear.

Three checks came back clear, and one didn't: too much leavening. That fits the symptom exactly — the extra gas formed and escaped faster than the structure could trap it, so the cake ballooned up fast, then fell once the bubbles burst, leaving the bitter edge behind from leavener that never got to react cleanly (King Arthur Baking; Scientific Baking). The fix next time: measure level, and trust the recipe's amount rather than adding "a little extra for safety."

Summary 2 min

  • A cake rises from three things together: chemical gas (soda or powder), steam and beaten-in air, and a protein structure that sets and holds the shape.
  • Baking soda is a base that only releases gas when the recipe supplies enough acid, such as buttermilk or cocoa.
  • Baking powder already carries its own acid and usually reacts twice: once on mixing, once on oven heat (double-acting).
  • Soda and powder aren't interchangeable 1-for-1 — soda is roughly three times stronger.
  • Too much leavener, underbaking, a mistaken oven temperature, or opening the door too early are more common causes of sinking than too little leavener.
