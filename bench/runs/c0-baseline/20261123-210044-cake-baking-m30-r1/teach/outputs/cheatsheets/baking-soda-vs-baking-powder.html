Baking soda vs. baking powder

 Baking sodaBaking powder
What it isA base (sodium bicarbonate) on its ownBaking soda + a dry acid + starch, premixed
Needs to reactAn acid already in the batterNothing extra; carries its own acid
ReactsOnce, right awayTwice: at mixing, and again from oven heat
Strength3–4× stronger, gram for gramWeaker; recipes use more of it
Recipe clueButtermilk, yogurt, brown sugar, cocoa, lemon juice nearbyPlain milk and flour, nothing especially acidic
Common swap1 tsp soda → 3 tsp powder1 tsp powder → ¼ tsp soda + ½ tsp cream of tartar

Diagnosing a cake that didn't rise right

Flat and dense all over (too little lift):

  1. Test the leavener: baking soda should fizz briskly in vinegar; baking powder should fizz in hot water.
  2. Check it was measured by spooning and leveling, not scooped, and not left out.
  3. For baking soda, check the batter has an acidic ingredient (buttermilk, yogurt, brown sugar, cocoa, lemon juice) for it to react with.

Rose, then sank in the middle (too much lift, or underbaked):

  1. Check the leavener amount was measured precisely, not heaping; too much creates more gas than the structure can hold.
  2. Check the center with a toothpick before pulling the cake out: wet batter means it needed more time.
  3. Check whether the oven door was opened before the center had set; a sudden temperature drop can collapse a half-set middle.
Not a 1:1 swap. Baking soda and baking powder are not interchangeable in equal amounts: baking soda is several times stronger, and each needs a different partner (an added acid, or nothing at all) to work.
Before adding more. A flat cake usually means a stale or mismeasured leavener, not a recipe that needed more. Test freshness and re-measure before increasing the amount next time.
