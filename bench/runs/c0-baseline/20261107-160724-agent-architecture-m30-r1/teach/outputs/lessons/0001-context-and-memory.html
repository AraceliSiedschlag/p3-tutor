What the assistant sees, and what it remembers

By the end of this lesson you can decide what your makerspace assistant keeps in its context, what it saves between sessions, and how it finds the right page in a long manual.

Objective O3: context and memory — the context window, what's kept between steps and between sessions, and retrieval and notes as memory About 30 minutes Builds on the agent loop from your last session
Why this matters for you

Your assistant has to answer from long machine manuals and remember each student's training status between visits. Both of those are context and memory problems, and they are two of the things your mission says you need to be able to decide. Get this wrong and the assistant either forgets things it should know, or burns your small model budget stuffing manuals into every request.

Warm-up 2 min

Recall

From your last session: a language model only knows what it learned in training, plus one other thing. What is that other thing, and why does it matter for a model that has never seen your machine manuals?

Check your answer

Plus whatever text it is given as input, right there in the request. A model that has never seen your manuals can still answer questions about them — but only if the relevant words from those manuals are included in what it's given. That "what it's given" is exactly what this lesson is about: it has a name (the context window), a size limit, and a cost, and deciding what goes into it is most of your design job.

The context window 5 min

Imagine texting a brilliant friend who has no short-term memory: the moment you send a text, they read it, reply, and forget everything, including your name. To keep a conversation going, you'd have to resend the whole exchange so far — every earlier text, the question, all of it — every single time you want a reply. That friend is, mechanically, what a language model call is like. There's nothing stored on "their side" between messages; whatever they can act on has to be inside the message you just sent.

The text inside one of those messages is chopped into pieces called tokens before the model processes it: a token is the smallest unit of text the model works with, and for English it's usually a word, a piece of a word, or a punctuation mark — roughly 3.5 to 4 characters each (Claude Platform Docs glossary). The context window is the set of tokens the model can see and act on in one single call: the system prompt that sets up its role, the tool definitions it can choose from, the conversation and tool-result history so far, the current question, and the reply it's about to write (Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents"). Nothing outside that window exists for the model at that moment, no matter how "recently" it happened.

Every one of those tokens, in and out, is billed. That's why "just paste the whole manual in, every time, to be safe" is a cost decision as much as a design one — on a small sponsor budget, it adds up fast.

Put a rough number on that. A page of running text in a machine manual is a few hundred words, which at roughly four characters per token works out to a few hundred tokens a page. A 40-page laser-cutter manual, pasted in whole, is easily 15,000 to 20,000 tokens — bigger than most entire conversations with students put together. Paste that in on every question, even ones about the calendar, and you're paying laser-cutter-manual prices to ask what time the makerspace closes.

What's kept within a session 5 min

Recall the agent loop from last time: the model picks a tool, the program runs it, the result comes back, and this repeats. Here's the piece that matters today: each time the model is called again, it doesn't just see the newest tool result — the program resends the entire history so far, growing turn after turn (Claude Platform Docs, "Context windows"). That's the only reason a multi-step answer feels continuous: the "memory" of step one is really just step one's text, copied back in at step four.

Say a student asks, "Is the laser cutter free Friday, and am I trained on it?" By the third model call in that one session, the context window holds: the system prompt and tools, the student's question, the check_calendar result, and the check_training_record result — all three, not just the latest one. That's what "kept within a session" means: everything accumulates, call after call, until the final answer is written.

Think of it the way you'd think about a Python loop that appends to a list instead of overwriting a variable. If step one sends 500 tokens and gets back a 200-token result, step two doesn't send 200 tokens — it sends the full 700, plus whatever step two adds. Step three sends that total again, plus its own addition. You already know what a running sum of ever-larger numbers does to a total: it's the same shape here, except the "total" is what you're billed for, every single step, for the rest of the session.

A common mix-up

If the history just keeps growing anyway, why not paste the whole 40-page manual in at step one, "to be safe"? It's tempting because more information feels like it can only help. But as token count grows, the model's ability to accurately find and use any one fact in that pile goes down, not up — a well-documented effect called context rot (Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents"). And because the whole history resends on every later step, those 40 pages get paid for again, and again, and again, for the rest of the session. Curating what goes in — the smallest set of tokens that actually answers the question — beats dumping everything in.

Between sessions: what resets to zero 6 min

Now stretch the same idea across a gap: the student leaves, and comes back next week with a new question. A new session means a brand-new context window, built from scratch: system prompt, tools, and this week's question. None of last week's conversation is in it, because nobody resent it — there's no "it" sitting anywhere inside the model waiting to be remembered.

A common mix-up

This is the misconception already flagged as M3: that the assistant automatically remembers earlier conversations, because it feels like "the same" assistant every time. It's an easy trap, because chat apps paper over the seam so well that the illusion of a continuous memory is convincing. But nothing is automatic: a fresh session starts with an empty context window. If the assistant is supposed to know that a student finished laser-cutter training last month, that fact has to have been written down somewhere outside the model, and read back in before this week's session can use it.

That "written down somewhere" is memory: information the program deliberately saves outside the context window — a file, a database row, a note — so a later session can read it back in instead of starting from nothing. Anthropic calls the technique of an agent regularly writing out what it has learned agentic memory, or note-taking, and it's exactly how a long-running agent keeps state across resets it can't otherwise see past (Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents").

For your assistant, that might be as plain as one row per student: {"student": "Jess", "laser_cutter_trained": "2026-09-12"}, written once when training is confirmed. Next session, before the model is even asked anything, your program looks up Jess's row and puts just that line into the new context window. The model never "remembered" it across the gap — your program fetched it and handed it over again, the same way it hands over a tool result.

Per-student facts aren't the only kind of note worth keeping. Anthropic's own write-up describes an agent that played a long game by keeping a running, saved tally of its progress across thousands of steps, instead of trying to hold the whole history in context (Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents"). Your assistant could do the same with its own operating notes — "the band saw's manual is missing page 4, flag it when asked" — written once by you or the assistant, and read back in every session from then on, instead of being rediscovered, or forgotten, each time.

Retrieval: finding the page instead of carrying the manual 5 min

A student asks, "Do I need a fume extractor to cut acrylic on the laser cutter?" The answer is one paragraph on page 12 of a 40-page manual. You have two options: paste all 40 pages into context on every question that might be manual-related, or search the manual first and paste in only the paragraph that matches.

The second option is retrieval: searching a larger store of text you keep outside the context window, and pulling in only the parts relevant to the current question, instead of holding everything in context all the time (Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents"). Mechanically, it's nothing new to you: it's usually just another tool, like check_calendar from last session's lesson. The model calls something like search_manuals("acrylic fume extraction"), your program searches the manual text (by keyword or by meaning) and returns the one matching paragraph as the tool result, and that paragraph joins the context the same way any tool result does.

The search itself can work two ways. A simple version matches words: it finds "fume extractor" because those words appear on page 12. A more flexible version compares meaning instead of exact wording, using a representation of text called an embedding, so a student asking "do I need ventilation for acrylic" still finds the same paragraph, even though they never typed "fume extractor." You don't need to build the second kind to start, and the math behind embeddings is out of scope here — but from the model's point of view, both look identical: a tool call goes out, a chunk of text comes back.

Retrieval and memory solve different problems, even though both keep context small. Retrieval searches a large, mostly unchanging store (the manuals) at the moment you need a piece of it. Memory saves a small, specific fact (this student passed this training) so it survives a session boundary the model could never see across on its own.

Worked example and practice 6 min

Worked example

A returning student asks: "Is the laser cutter free Friday, and have I done the training?"

  1. Session starts, context is small: system prompt, tool definitions, and the student's question. Nothing from any earlier visit is in there yet.
  2. Model calls check_calendar(laser_cutter, friday): the program runs it and appends the result. Context now holds the question and this result.
  3. Model calls check_training_record(student_id): the program looks up a row that was written weeks ago, in a past session, right after the student passed the safety quiz. That saved record is memory doing its job: a fact from a session that ended long ago, read back into a context window that otherwise has no idea that session ever happened.
  4. Model writes the final answer using everything now in context: the calendar result and the training record.
  5. Session ends; the context window is discarded. Nothing from this conversation persists except what was already durably saved before it started — the training row, untouched by today's visit.
Your turn

A student asks the assistant to recommend a 3D-printer filament for a part that needs to flex, and mentions, for the first time, that they're allergic to the fumes from one material (ABS). The filament comparison is a table on page 7 of a 50-page manual. Step 1 is given: the new session's context starts with the system prompt, the tool definitions, and this question (with the allergy mentioned right there in it). Work out the rest: should the model paste in the whole 50-page manual, or retrieve just page 7? And should the allergy be used only for this answer, or also saved for next time?

Show a solution

Retrieve, don't paste: a search_manuals("filament flexibility comparison")-style call returns the page 7 table, which is enough to answer, at a fraction of the tokens (and the context-rot risk) of 50 pages.

Save the allergy, don't just use it once: it's exactly the kind of durable, per-student fact this lesson's memory idea is for — without saving it, the student has to repeat it every visit, and a session that skips asking could recommend ABS by accident. One sentence of design judgment worth raising at your review, though: saving health-related facts about a named student is also a privacy decision, not just a technical one, so it deserves its own line in whatever policy governs your student records — not a silent default.

Summary 2 min

  • A model call only ever sees its context window: the system prompt, tool definitions, the history resent so far, and the current question — nothing else, no matter how recent (M3).
  • Within a session, the agent loop keeps resending the whole growing history, which is why it feels continuous; but that growth costs more tokens and, past a point, costs accuracy too (context rot, M2).
  • Between sessions, the context window is thrown away completely. Anything the assistant should "remember" has to be written to a file or database on purpose, and read back in next time — that's memory.
  • Retrieval keeps a big, mostly static store (your manuals) outside context and pulls in just the relevant piece on demand, usually through an ordinary tool call.

Say it in your own words: explain to a teammate why your assistant needs both a search tool for the manuals and a saved record for each student's training status — and why neither one is the same thing as "just give it a bigger context window."
