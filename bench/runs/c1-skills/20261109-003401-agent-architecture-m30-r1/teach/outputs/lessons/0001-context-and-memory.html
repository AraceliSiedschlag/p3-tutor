Context and memory: what your agent actually remembers

By the end of this lesson you can say, for any point in your makerspace assistant's work, exactly what the model can see right now, what survives into the next step, and what has to be deliberately saved to survive into tomorrow.

Objective O3: context window, what carries across steps and sessions, retrieval and notes as memory About 30 minutes Builds on the agent loop from your last session
Why this matters for you

Your assistant has to answer from long machine manuals, check a calendar, and remember each student's training status between visits. All three of those are context-and-memory problems: how much of the manual it can "see" at once, whether a fact from Monday's chat is still around on Thursday, and how it finds the one page it needs without reading every page every time. Get this wrong and the assistant either forgets things it should remember, or burns your sponsor's budget re-reading the whole manual on every question.

It hands the result back to the model as part of the next input, and calls the model again. That's the detail we're about to pull apart today: "part of the next input" means the result gets added to the pile of text the model reads at the next step — and that pile is exactly what today's lesson is about.

The context window 5 min

Every time the model is called, it is handed one pile of text and asked to produce more text. That whole pile — the system instructions, the tool descriptions, the conversation so far, and whatever just came back from a tool — is called its context. The context window is the maximum size that pile is allowed to be, measured in tokens, the small chunks of text (roughly three-quarters of a word each) the model actually reads and writes (IBM, "What Is Context Engineering?").

Think of it like your kitchen counter while you cook. The counter has a fixed size. Everything you need for the dish in front of you — the recipe, the ingredients you've pulled out, a sticky note about a substitution — has to fit on that counter at once. You can't spread out onto a counter in a different room; if it isn't on this counter, it isn't something you can currently see or use.

Two things follow directly from this, and both matter for your sponsor's budget:

  • The window is shared between what goes in and what comes out. The question, the tool results, and the model's own answer all draw from the same token budget in that one call.
  • Every token, in or out, is one you pay for. A tool that dumps a whole 80-page manual into context costs roughly eighty times what a tool that returns one relevant page costs — same question, same model, very different bill.

For your assistant specifically, "system instructions" is the short description of its job and rules (something like "you help students check machine availability and policy; always confirm training before suggesting a booking"), and "tool descriptions" are the write-ups of check_calendar, search_manuals, and check_training_record that tell the model what each one does and what arguments it takes — the same descriptions you practiced writing last lesson. Both of those sit in context on every single call, question after question, all day. That's worth knowing, because it means keeping them tight isn't a one-time nicety; it's a cost you pay on every request your assistant ever handles.

Why not just build models with a counter the size of a warehouse, so this never comes up? Bigger windows exist and keep growing, but a bigger counter is also a slower, pricier one to work from for every single call, even a trivial one — and, as you'll see in the next section, a bigger pile of text is not automatically a more accurate one. Sizing the counter to the dish, not the kitchen, is the actual skill.

What carries between steps, in one session 6 min

Last time you traced the agent loop: the model picks an action, the program runs it, the result goes back in, and the model is called again. "Goes back in" means it is appended to the conversation-so-far box from Figure 1. So within one session, the context window doesn't reset between steps — it accumulates. Step 2's call sees step 1's tool result. Step 5's call sees everything from steps 1 through 4, plus whatever step 5 just got back.

That sounds like a feature, and it is, up to a point. The problem is that your kitchen counter from Figure 1 only gets fuller as the session goes on, and a fuller counter is a worse one to cook from. Anthropic's engineering team calls this context rot: "as the number of tokens in the context window increases, the model's ability to accurately recall information from that context decreases" (Anthropic, "Effective context engineering for AI agents"). A fact that was crystal clear on a mostly-empty counter can get lost once the counter is covered in fifteen earlier tool results.

This is also why that "step limit" safeguard from your last quiz matters for more than just runaway loops: every extra step is also extra tokens sitting in a window that's already getting crowded.
A common mix-up

"A bigger context window means a more reliable agent." It's tempting, because a bigger window obviously lets you cram in more. But cramming in more is exactly what causes context rot: more tokens in the pile, including irrelevant leftovers from five steps ago, makes it harder for the model to find and weight the one fact that actually matters right now. A smaller, well-curated context usually beats a huge, cluttered one. (This is a new misconception for this workspace — we'll call it M7.)

You don't have to accept the growth passively. The same source describes agents that periodically "cyclically refine" what carries forward — summarizing older tool results, dropping ones that turned out to be dead ends, and keeping only what's still load-bearing. That's a design choice your assistant's program has to make on purpose; the model itself won't tidy the counter for you.

Concretely, for your assistant that might mean: once check_calendar and search_manuals have both run and the model has used their results to decide an answer, the program replaces those two raw results with one short line it writes itself, like "Laser cutter: Friday open, training required." The next step reads that one line instead of the original page-and-a-half of calendar JSON and manual text. Same underlying facts, a fraction of the tokens, and nothing load-bearing lost. This is a judgment call every time — compress too eagerly and you can lose a detail the model needed; don't compress at all and you're back in the rot zone from Figure 2.

What does not carry between sessions 6 min

Everything in the last section was about one session — one continuous back-and-forth, like one visit to the makerspace. When that visit ends, the whole context window is thrown away. Anthropic puts it plainly: "between separate sessions, agents employ dedicated memory strategies rather than relying on context persistence. The accumulated conversation is typically cleared" (Anthropic). The IBM piece frames the same thing from the other side: the context window is "the model's short-term memory" (IBM) — short-term, not long-term.

Picture Riley the student, not Riley the designer: Riley asks your assistant on Monday whether they need training for the laser cutter, and the assistant walks them through it. Riley comes back Thursday and asks if the laser cutter is free. Unless your program did something deliberate in between, Thursday's session starts from a completely empty counter. The model does not "remember Riley" the way a shopkeeper who's seen you every week does. It is called fresh, with no idea Monday happened, unless something outside the chat handed it a note.

It's easy to miss this because plenty of chat apps feel continuous across a whole day. That feeling is usually the app quietly re-sending the earlier messages as part of the next call's context — still Figure 1's one pile, just assembled by the program before the model ever sees it. The moment that app is closed and reopened days later, or a different session is started, there's nothing left to resend unless the facts were written down somewhere that survives. Continuity you can see in a chat window is a choice someone's program made, not a property of the model.

A common mix-up

This is the flip side of M3 from your last quiz — the wrong option that said an agent gets "automatic memory of every earlier conversation." It's an easy thing to believe, because a good chatbot feels continuous, the same way a good magic trick feels seamless. But nothing is automatic. If your assistant seems to remember Riley finished training, it's because some part of your program — not the model — wrote that fact down somewhere and loaded it back in on Thursday. No write, no memory. That's true whether "remembering" means a fact about a student or a fact about a machine.

Retrieval and notes: the two ways memory crosses the gap 6 min

So if nothing crosses between sessions by default, and nothing crosses between steps for free either once the counter is full, how does a well-built agent ever seem to "remember" anything? Two patterns, and your makerspace assistant needs both.

Pattern 1: notes, the write-it-down memory

The program writes small, structured facts to storage that lives outside the context window — a file, a database row, a log — and then deliberately loads the relevant bit back in at the start of a later session. Anthropic's own stress test of this was an agent playing Pokémon for thousands of steps, far more than any context window could hold; it survived by maintaining a running note like "for the last 1,234 steps I've been training my Pikachu on Route 1, and it's gained 8 levels" (Anthropic). Translate that to your assistant: instead of a level-up log, it's a training record — "Riley: laser cutter safety training completed 2026-11-02." That one line, read back in on Thursday, is Riley's whole memory. It is cheap, because it's small, and reliable, because it's just a fact, not a hope that the model "recalls" something from a discarded session.

Pattern 2: retrieval, the look-it-up memory

For something too big to ever write as one tidy note — your 80-page machine manual — the trick is different. The program doesn't pre-load the whole thing. It keeps "lightweight identifiers (file paths, stored queries, web links, etc.)" and fetches the one relevant piece only when it's actually needed, the way you'd use a table of contents or an index instead of reading a cookbook cover to cover every time you want one recipe (Anthropic). This is the same shape as the search_manuals tool from your last lesson: the model doesn't have the manual memorized, and it never "has" more than the one chunk a search call hands it.

Anthropic calls the broader version of this progressive disclosure: the agent "incrementally discovers relevant context through exploration, with each interaction informing subsequent decisions" — it mirrors how you'd actually use a cookbook, checking the index, skimming a candidate page, and only then deciding whether to read the recipe in full or flip further. For your assistant, that can mean a first, broad search ("laser cutter safety") followed by a narrower one ("laser cutter ventilation") once the first result hints at where the real answer lives, rather than betting everything on one guess at the search terms. A search tool is only as good as the queries it's given; a vague query returns a vague page, and the model will build its answer on whatever it got, confidently, whether or not it was the right page.

A common mix-up

"If the assistant can search the manual, it has effectively read the whole manual." It hasn't, and this one bites hard. The model only ever sees whatever chunk the search tool actually returns — call it M8. If the search misses the page about ventilation requirements, the model has no fallback sense that "there was probably more in there." It will answer fluently from the one page it got, with the same confident tone it would use if that page were the whole truth. (That's the same root problem from your very first lesson: the model produces plausible text from what it was given, whether or not that's the complete picture.) Good retrieval design — searching well, fetching a bit more context around a match — is what keeps this from biting your students.

Worked example and practice 3 min

Worked example

Trace Riley across two visits.

  1. Monday, session 1: Riley asks "Can I use the laser cutter Friday, and do I need training?" The agent loop runs: check_calendar (Friday is open), search_manuals (training is required), check_training_record (none on file). All three results pile into this one session's context window, just like Figure 1 and 2. The model gives its final answer: book-able, but sign up for training first. Session ends; the whole window is discarded, as in Figure 3.
  2. Monday afternoon, outside any chat: Riley completes training in person. A staff member (or a form the assistant's program exposes) writes one line to storage: "Riley: laser cutter training completed 2026-11-02." This is Pattern 1 — a note, written outside the context window entirely.
  3. Thursday, session 2: a brand-new, empty context window, exactly as Figure 3 shows. Riley asks "Is the laser cutter free today?" The agent calls check_training_record, which retrieves that one saved line — not Monday's conversation, just the fact — and check_calendar. The model answers: yes, and you're cleared to use it. Nothing about Monday's chat ever re-entered context; only the one note did.

Notice the cost shape across the two sessions: Thursday's whole context is a couple of tool descriptions, one short note, and one calendar check — cheap, because the design did the work of keeping it small, not because the question was simple.

A student used the 3D printer last week and left a note in the shared logbook that the extruder was clogged and got cleared. Today, a different student asks your assistant whether the 3D printer is working. Start the same way the worked example did: what's the first thing that has to happen before the model can answer well, and which pattern (note or retrieval) is it? Then say why loading the entire logbook's history into context for this one question would be a bad move.

Show a solution

First, the assistant needs to get at the logbook's most recent relevant entry for this machine — that's retrieval if the logbook is long and the program fetches just the latest clog-related entry, or it could be treated as a note if the program keeps one up-to-date "status" line per machine instead of a full log. Either way, the model cannot answer well with an empty context window; something has to be fetched or read back in first, exactly as Thursday's session needed the training record. Loading the whole logbook's history would be wasteful for the same reason Figure 2 warns about: most of those entries are irrelevant to "is it working right now," they'd cost tokens on every future question about any machine, and burying today's one relevant line under old entries risks the model missing it — context rot, not better memory.

Summary 1 min

  • The context window is the one pile of tokens the model sees on a single call — input and output share it, and every token in that pile costs you.
  • Within a session, context only grows across steps; left unmanaged, that growth causes context rot (M7: bigger context isn't automatically better).
  • Between sessions, nothing carries over by default — the whole window is discarded (the flip side of M3: nothing is remembered automatically).
  • Notes (small saved facts, written outside context and read back later) and retrieval (fetching just the relevant chunk of something too big to carry) are the two patterns that bridge that gap — and retrieval only ever hands the model the chunk it fetched, never the whole source (M8).
